London has always hidden its truths beneath its silence.

Some are buried beneath blood and ash.

And some should have never been remembered.

This is only the beginning.

Chapter One: The City Beneath the Clock

Published in London Times

The bells rang seven times.

Their deep, solemn echoes drifted across London, rolling over rooftops and slipping through the thick morning fog that swallowed the city whole.

To strangers, London was magnificent.

Grand buildings stood proudly beneath the gray sky. Carriages rattled across cobblestone streets. Merchants prepared their stalls while smoke curled from countless chimneys.

From a distance, it looked like the greatest city in the world.

But distance was a liar.

Beneath the beauty hid corruption. Beneath the wealth hid greed. Beneath every smiling face hid a secret waiting for the right moment to emerge.

London was a city that wore masks.

And no one understood that better than the people who lived at its bottom.

Far from the noble districts and luxurious estates stood a forgotten corner of the city—a place ignored by politicians and abandoned by opportunity.

The orphan quarter.

Its buildings were old. Its streets were narrow. Rainwater collected in broken stone pathways, and poverty clung to every wall like a permanent stain.

Among those streets walked a young man with a worn brown coat and a pocket watch hanging from his vest.

His name was Ronan.

And at that particular moment, he was chasing a cat.

"Stop! Come back here!"

The orange cat darted through an alleyway.

A basket of apples overturned as it passed.

A merchant immediately shouted in protest.

"My apples!"

"Sorry!" Ronan called back. "I'll pay for them later!"

The cat leaped over a wooden fence.

Ronan followed.

After several exhausting minutes, he finally cornered the animal beside a stack of crates.

The cat hissed.

Ronan sighed.

"You're causing more trouble than half the people in this city."

He carefully picked it up.

Moments later, a small girl came running down the street.

"Mr Ronan!"

The relief on her face was immediate.

Ronan handed over the cat.

The little girl hugged it tightly.

"Thank you!"

"You should watch him more carefully."

"I will."

"You said that last time."

The girl laughed.

"So you'll help me again if he runs away?"

Ronan smiled.

"Probably."

The girl grinned before running home.

Ronan watched her disappear into the crowd.

A small smile remained on his face.

That was the kind of work he usually received.

Lost pets.

Missing belongings.

Neighborhood disputes.

Nothing exciting.

Nothing dangerous.

Certainly nothing worthy of the title detective.

The government didn't consider him one.

Most officials barely knew he existed.

But Ronan didn't mind.

Helping people was enough.

At least, it used to be.

The city grew busier as the day passed.

Workers hurried through the streets.

Merchants argued with customers.

Children laughed while chasing each other between market stalls.

For a brief moment, everything felt normal.

Then a carriage appeared.

Unlike the others, this one immediately commanded attention.

Its black exterior gleamed despite the cloudy sky.

A silver crest decorated its side.

The reaction was instant.

People stepped aside.

Conversations stopped.

Heads lowered.

Ronan watched silently as the carriage rolled past.

Even from a distance, he recognized the emblem.

Ashford.

One of the most influential noble families in London.

Their wealth stretched across the kingdom.

Their influence reached places ordinary citizens could never imagine.

People respected them.

People feared them.

And people never questioned them.

The carriage disappeared into the fog.

Life resumed.

Ronan reached into his coat and removed the old pocket watch he carried everywhere.

The silver casing was scratched.

Its chain had lost its shine years ago.

Yet the watch continued to tick faithfully.

Tick. Tick. Tick.

For reasons he couldn't explain, the sound always comforted him.

As if it reminded him that no matter how difficult life became, time continued moving forward.

The sky darkened.

Rain began to fall.

Street lamps flickered awake one by one.

The city prepared for another ordinary evening.

Then the screaming started.

At first, nobody understood what was happening.

A woman rushed through the street.

Several guards followed behind her.

Their faces were pale.

Their movements frantic.

People immediately gathered.

"What happened?"

"Is there a fire?"

"No."

The woman looked as though she had seen a ghost.

Her voice trembled.

"A body."

The crowd fell silent.

"A body was found near London Bridge."

A chill ran through Ronan.

Something about the woman's expression felt wrong.

This wasn't ordinary fear.

It was horror.

Within minutes, rumors spread across the city.

People abandoned their work.

Shopkeepers left their stores.

Citizens flooded toward London Bridge.

Ronan followed.

The rain intensified.

The closer he got, the heavier the atmosphere became.

More guards appeared.

More barriers.

More frightened faces.

By the time he reached the bridge, dozens of officers had already secured the area.

Nobody was speaking.

Nobody dared.

Ronan slowly moved through the crowd.

Then he saw it.

The body.

For a moment, he couldn't breathe.

The victim lay motionless on the wet stone.

The brutality was unlike anything he had ever witnessed.

Even experienced guards looked disturbed.

One officer turned away and vomited.

Another stared in stunned silence.

Ronan felt his stomach tighten.

Whoever had done this hadn't simply wanted to kill.

They had wanted to send a message.

Then he noticed something beside the corpse.

A single playing card.

Rainwater flowed across its surface.

Yet the image remained perfectly visible.

A Fool.

The card rested silently beside the dead man.

As if it had been placed there intentionally.

As if it were a signature.

Thunder echoed above the city.

Far away, the great clock tower rang once more.

And standing among the crowd, staring at that strange card, Ronan felt a feeling he had never experienced before.

A feeling that something terrible had just entered London.

And that his life would never be the same again.

End of Chapter One 🕰️🎭📖

Chapter 2: The London Bridge Case

Published in London Times

18 July

By the time the first rays of sunlight touched the rooftops of London, the murder had become the only thing the city could talk about.

The streets were louder than usual. Not because of business. Not because of celebration. But because of fear.

Merchants discussed the incident while opening their shops. Workers argued about it before leaving for the factories. Coachmen exchanged rumors while waiting for passengers.

Even children whispered about the mysterious body found on London Bridge the previous night.

The newspapers had sold out before breakfast. Every headline carried the same story.

A Powerful Minister Found Dead on London Bridge.

Nobody knew the truth. Only rumors.

"I heard he was murdered before midnight."

"No... someone said it happened at dawn."

"They say the Royal Palace has already sealed the bridge."

"My cousin works near Westminster. He says the ministers haven't slept all night."

"And the killer... They say he left something beside the body. A playing card. The Fool."

Every conversation ended with silence. For the first time in years... London felt uneasy.

Far from Westminster... In one of London's poorest neighborhoods... A small wooden door slowly opened.

Ronan stepped outside. His home was barely enough for one person. A worn-out table. A single chair. A bookshelf overflowing with old case files. Nothing luxurious. Nothing unnecessary.

He quietly reached into his coat pocket. Three copper coins. He stared at them for a second before closing his hand. That was all he had. Breakfast... Or nothing.

The smell of freshly baked bread drifted through the morning air. Without saying a word, Ronan began walking toward the neighborhood bakery.

The bakery was already crowded. Fresh loaves filled the shelves. Golden crusts. Soft white bread. Warm rolls fresh from the oven.

Ronan looked at them only briefly. Then his eyes moved toward a small basket beneath the counter. Yesterday's bread. Hard. Dry. Cheap.

The old baker looked up and smiled. "Morning, Ronan."

"Morning."

Without asking any questions, the baker reached for the stale loaf. "The usual?"

Ronan nodded. "The usual."

For a moment... The baker glanced toward the fresh bread. His expression softened. He wanted to help. But another customer entered. A wealthy noblewoman dressed in expensive clothes.

The baker quietly placed the fresh loaf back where it belonged. He wrapped the stale bread instead. "I'm sorry."

Ronan smiled gently. "You don't have to be."

The baker placed the loaf on the counter. "Three copper."

Ronan emptied every coin he owned onto the wooden counter. The metallic sound echoed through the quiet shop. The baker accepted the payment.

As Ronan turned to leave... The old man spoke. "One day... You'll be buying the fresh ones."

Ronan looked back with a faint smile. "I hope so."

He stepped back into the streets of London. The loaf of bread rested beneath his arm. It wasn't heavy. Yet somehow... It felt like he was carrying the weight of his entire life.

He disappeared into the morning crowd... While behind him, London continued whispering about the murder that had changed everything.

Ronan walked slowly through the narrow streets of East London. The stale loaf rested beneath his arm while the city around him continued discussing the murder.

No one noticed him. That was something he had grown accustomed to long ago. To most people, he was not a detective. He was simply another poor man in a worn-out coat. Another commoner trying to survive the day.

As he passed a small marketplace, two well-dressed noble boys stepped out of a carriage. One of them accidentally brushed against Ronan's shoulder. The boy immediately stepped back as though he had touched something filthy.

"Watch where you're going."

His friend pulled out a white handkerchief and dusted off the sleeve of his expensive coat. "Leave him. They're commoners. They don't know any better."

The boys laughed quietly before walking away. Ronan did not answer. He did not even turn around.

Insults had stopped hurting him years ago. Growing up as an orphan had taught him a cruel lesson very early in life—

The world was not divided between good people and bad people. It was divided between those with power... ...and those without it.

A few minutes later, he reached the end of the street. An old building stood there, its paint faded and its wooden sign barely hanging above the entrance.

St. Gabriel's Orphanage.

Ronan stopped walking. The sight pulled him backward through time. He could almost hear the voices of children arguing over scraps of food. He could almost feel the cold stone floor beneath his feet.

Memories returned one after another. Carrying sacks of rice that were heavier than he was. Selling newspapers before sunrise. Polishing strangers' boots for a single copper coin. Unloading carts until his hands bled.

Some days he earned enough to eat. Some days he did not. No one had protected him. No one had rescued him. He survived because he learned how to keep moving forward.

Ronan smiled faintly at the memory. "If I had chosen factory work," he murmured to himself, "I would probably have more money today."

He looked down at the detective badge hidden beneath his coat. "But I wouldn't be able to help people."

That thought alone was enough. He turned away from the orphanage and continued toward home.

By the time he reached his small house, the morning sun had climbed higher into the sky. Ronan unlocked the door and stepped inside.

The room was quiet. Too quiet. He placed the loaf on the table and removed his coat. For the first time that morning, he allowed himself to sit down.

His eyes drifted toward the stack of investigation files beside the window. The London Bridge murder refused to leave his thoughts. The blood. The wounds. The terror frozen on the victim's face.

Ronan closed his eyes for a moment. Then came a knock at the door.

Knock. Knock. Knock.

A familiar voice called from outside. "Ronan? You'd better open the door before I break it down."

A small smile appeared on his face despite himself. "Evelyn."

He crossed the room and opened the door. Standing outside was a young woman in a dark coat, a leather notebook tucked beneath one arm. Chestnut hair framed her face, and her eyes carried the same sharp curiosity they had carried since childhood.

"So," she said, folding her arms, "you really forgot about me?"

Ronan chuckled softly. "I was hoping you had forgotten where I live."

Evelyn rolled her eyes. "After twenty years? Impossible."

Without waiting for permission, she stepped inside. Her gaze moved around the tiny room. The cracked walls. The old table. The overflowing bookshelf. The single chair. Nothing had changed.

She sighed. "Ronan... this place still looks exactly the same."

Ronan shrugged. "I haven't had time to redecorate."

Evelyn noticed the loaf of bread wrapped in brown paper. She picked it up gently. "Yesterday's bread?"

Ronan remained silent. For a brief moment, something like sadness crossed her face. But she carefully placed the loaf back on the table without another comment.

Instead, she lifted a small paper bag from behind her back. "I brought coffee," she announced. "And before you say anything—no, you are not refusing it."

Ronan laughed quietly. "You always win these arguments."

"Of course I do."

They sat across from each other, and for a few peaceful minutes neither of them mentioned the murder. The silence felt familiar. Comfortable. Like two people who had known each other long before London learned their names.

Finally, Evelyn opened her notebook. "The whole city is talking about London Bridge," she said softly.

Ronan nodded. "I noticed."

"The London Times hasn't covered a story this big in years." She leaned forward slightly. "And Ronan... the Royal Palace is hiding something."

Ronan looked at her carefully. "You sound very certain."

Evelyn smiled faintly. "I have sources."

Outside, distant church bells echoed through the streets of London as the city continued whispering about the murder that had changed everything.

For a brief moment... Neither of them spoke. The room remained silent until loud voices echoed from outside.

"RONAN! Come out already! We're going to be late!"

Evelyn smiled. "They're here."

Ronan shook his head with a small laugh. "My daily headache."

He opened the door. Standing outside were two young men wearing dusty factory uniforms. One was tall and broad-shouldered. The other looked younger, carrying his coat over one shoulder.

Samuel Brooks. Oliver Hayes. Both had been Ronan's closest friends since childhood.

Samuel grinned the moment he saw him. "There he is. The world's poorest detective."

Oliver laughed. "And somehow still richer than us in respect."

Ronan folded his arms. "You two only come here when you want something."

Samuel pointed toward the street. "We're done with work early today. So... You're coming with us."

"Where?"

"The tavern."

Ronan sighed. "I don't have money."

Samuel rolled his eyes. "You never do."

Oliver laughed. "That's why we're paying."

Before Ronan could refuse... Samuel grabbed his shoulder. "No excuses. You've been locked inside this house since yesterday. You need fresh air."

Evelyn stood up, closing her notebook. "I'll come too."

Samuel smiled. "Perfect. Now he'll have no excuse."

A few minutes later... The four of them walked through London's crowded streets. Workers were returning from the factories. The smell of coal filled the air. Horse-drawn carriages rattled over the wet stone roads. Despite everything that had happened... Life in London refused to stop.

The tavern was warm. Dim lanterns hung from wooden beams. Workers laughed loudly over cheap whisky. A violinist played quietly in one corner.

Samuel immediately ordered drinks. Three glasses of whisky. One cup of black coffee.

Oliver frowned. "You still won't drink?"

Ronan picked up the coffee. "I like remembering what happened the next morning."

The table erupted with laughter. For a while... They forgot about the murder. They talked about work. Old memories. Childhood. Life.

Then Evelyn suddenly placed today's newspaper on the table. The headline immediately silenced everyone.

LONDON BRIDGE MURDER SHOCKS THE KINGDOM

Samuel looked toward her. "So... What are people saying now?"

Evelyn crossed her arms. "The city thinks this is just another murder." She paused. "The Royal Palace doesn't."

Everyone became quiet.

"I've been speaking with a few sources this morning. They told me something interesting. The Palace spent the entire night arguing. They're trying to decide who should handle the investigation."

Oliver leaned forward. "So they're finally doing something."

"They have to. The victim wasn't an ordinary noble. He represented the kingdom in foreign affairs. If other nations hear about this... The King's reputation will suffer."

Samuel lowered his voice. "So who are they assigning?"

Evelyn glanced around before speaking. "They've already selected one of the kingdom's most respected forensic examiners. Dr. Theodore Graves. And an honest police inspector. Inspector Elias Ward."

Ronan listened quietly. "What makes them different?"

"They can't be bought."

Samuel let out a low whistle. "So the Palace actually wants the truth?"

Evelyn slowly shook her head. "No. They want control. If the public discovers what Lord Beaumont was truly involved in... The kingdom could lose faith in the nobles. They're trying to stop panic before it begins."

Oliver frowned. "So what now?"

Evelyn looked directly at Ronan. "My source also said something else. The ministers are looking for someone... ...who has no ties to noble families. ...someone the public already trusts. ...someone independent."

Ronan remained silent. For some reason... That sentence stayed in his mind.

Outside... Thunder rolled across London's sky. Rain began striking the tavern windows. Ronan stared quietly into his untouched coffee. Then... Something flashed through his memory. His eyes widened.

The bridge. The body. The blood. And... Something else. Something he had ignored.

He slowly stood up. Samuel frowned. "Ronan?"

He didn't answer. "I missed something."

The table fell silent. "What do you mean?"

Ronan looked toward the rain outside. "There was something near the crime scene. Not beside the body... Near the edge of the bridge."

Without another word... He grabbed his coat and rushed toward the tavern door.

Evelyn immediately stood. "Ronan!"

The tavern door slammed shut behind Ronan. Cold rain immediately soaked his coat. He ignored it. His heart pounded faster with every step.

The streets blurred around him as he ran through the darkness. People shouted. Carriages rushed past. The city continued moving. But Ronan heard none of it. Only one thought echoed inside his mind.

The bridge.

He remembered standing beside Lord Beaumont's body. He remembered the blood. The Royal Guards. The growing crowd. And then... Something near the edge of the bridge. Something he had dismissed in the chaos.

He should have checked. He should have looked closer.

Behind him, Evelyn called out his name, but the rain swallowed her voice.

Within minutes... London Bridge emerged through the thick blanket of fog. The crime scene had already been abandoned. The Guards were gone. Only silence remained.

Rainwater slowly washed away the last traces of blood. Ronan walked toward the exact place where Lord Beaumont's body had been discovered. His breathing slowed. Every detail returned to him.

Ten feet away... Near the old stone railing. There.

He knelt. His fingers searched carefully between the cracks in the weathered stones. Nothing. He searched again. Still nothing.

Then... His fingertips brushed against something thin. Paper. Carefully... He pulled it free.

A single playing card. The Fool.

His heartbeat stopped. It looked identical to the first one. But this one... Had something written on the back.

Ronan slowly turned the card over. Only one sentence.

«"You're looking in the wrong direction."»

For several long seconds... He couldn't move. The cold wind swept across London Bridge. His eyes instinctively searched the rooftops. The towers. The empty streets. The darkness hidden behind the fog.

Nothing. No footsteps. No movement. Only silence.

Yet... For the first time in his life... Ronan felt certain of one thing. The killer had never truly left. He had simply been watching. Watching the police. Watching the Royal Guards. Watching... Everyone.

A distant church bell echoed across London. One... Two... Three... Midnight.

Ronan carefully slipped the card inside his coat. His expression had completely changed. This was no longer just another murder investigation. Someone had planned every step. Someone had expected the authorities to overlook the second card. Someone wanted the investigation to begin exactly this way.

Far beyond the bridge... Hidden somewhere within London's endless fog... A lone figure stood motionless. For just a brief moment... A faint smile appeared beneath the shadow of a wide-brimmed hat. Then the figure quietly disappeared into the darkness.

Ronan remained standing on the bridge, the rain falling around him. He looked once more at the empty road stretching into the night. Somewhere in this city... A murderer was waiting.

And for the first time... Ronan realized he wasn't simply investigating a crime. He had stepped into someone else's plan.

End of Chapter 2

Chapter Three: The Royal Summons

Published in London Times

The morning after the murder of Lord Aldrich Beaumont, London woke beneath a sky of pale grey.

The rain had stopped sometime before dawn, but the city still carried the dampness of the night. Water clung to the stone streets, gathered along the edges of buildings, and reflected the weak morning light in broken pieces.

The city was awake. And it was talking.

Newspapers were being sold on every busy corner. Boys ran through the streets shouting headlines at the top of their voices.

“Lord Beaumont murdered!”

“Powerful minister found dead on London Bridge!”

“Another mystery surrounds the London Bridge murder!”

People stopped to listen. Shopkeepers leaned over their counters. Gentlemen gathered outside coffee houses. Carriage drivers discussed the murder with their passengers.

Even those who had never seen Lord Beaumont's face knew his name now.

But while London argued over the murder, the most powerful people in the kingdom had gathered behind closed doors.

Inside the Royal Palace, there was no shouting. No newspapers being sold. No crowds. Only silence.

The Palace stood in the heart of Westminster, enormous and imposing against the surrounding buildings. Its tall stone walls were still dark from the night's rain, while guards in dark uniforms stood at every entrance.

Inside, servants moved quietly through the long corridors. Their shoes made soft sounds against the polished floor. Every few minutes, another sealed document passed from one official to another.

The murder had reached the highest level of government. And this morning, the Royal Council had been called together.

The council chamber was one of the oldest rooms in the Palace. Tall windows covered one side of the room, their glass blurred by the moisture left behind by the rain. Heavy curtains hung beside them, dark red and perfectly still.

At the center stood a long table made from dark walnut. Seven chairs surrounded it. Seven seats. Seven Great Noble Clans.

For generations, these families had held enormous influence over the kingdom. Their names were known in Parliament, the military, the courts, and the highest circles of society.

One of those seats belonged to the Ashford Clan. Lord Alistair Ashford sat quietly in it. His silver hair was neatly combed, and his black coat was perfectly arranged. His expression was calm, almost emotionless. He listened.

Across from him sat Lord Beaumont. Unlike Alistair, he had made no attempt to hide his anger. His face was pale. His hands were tightly clenched on the table. In front of him lay the official report from London Bridge. He had read it several times. Still, it seemed impossible to accept.

His son was dead. Lord Aldrich Beaumont. A powerful minister. A member of one of the kingdom's most influential families. Murdered in the middle of London.

Lord Beaumont slowly lifted his eyes. “My son was murdered.” His voice was quiet. But the entire chamber heard him. Nobody replied.

“He was murdered in the center of the capital.” His fingers tightened against the table. “And whoever did it is still walking somewhere in this city.”

The Chancellor sitting at the head of the table looked at him. “We understand the seriousness of the situation, Lord Beaumont.”

Lord Beaumont's eyes hardened. “No.” The Chancellor paused. “You understand the political situation.”

The room became still.

“My son is dead,” Lord Beaumont continued. “That is my situation.” Nobody interrupted him. “I want the murderer found.” His voice grew colder. “And when he is found, I want him punished.”

One of the council members slowly placed his hands together. “The police are already investigating.”

“The police?” Lord Beaumont gave a humorless smile. “They have been investigating since last night. And what have they found?”

Silence. That silence was enough. Lord Beaumont leaned back in his chair. “That is what I thought.”

The Chancellor looked toward the senior investigation official standing near the end of the table. The man had been silent until now. He carried several documents under one arm. “Tell us the current situation.”

The official stepped forward. “Yes, Your Grace.” He placed the first document on the table. “Inspector Elias Ward is involved in the investigation.”

Lord Beaumont looked toward him. “Inspector Ward is an honest man.”

“He is.”

“Then let him continue.”

The official nodded. “He will.”

“Then what is the problem?”

The official looked around the room. “The problem is not Inspector Ward.” He placed another document beside the first. “The problem is the attention surrounding the case.”

Lord Beaumont frowned. “What attention?”

“The newspapers. The public. The political factions. And the noble families.” He paused. “Everyone wants answers.”

The Chancellor looked at the documents. “And they will get them.”

“Yes.” The official's voice remained calm. “But the more important question is…” He looked at the seven members of the council. “Who should be given responsibility for finding those answers?”

Nobody spoke for a moment. The official continued. “If we assign the investigation to a senior Palace investigator, every action will be questioned. If we place it entirely in the hands of the police, political pressure may interfere. If we appoint someone connected to one of the noble families, the investigation will immediately become a matter of suspicion.”

Lord Beaumont's expression darkened. “So what do you propose?”

The official reached beneath the other documents. He took out a thin brown file. Unlike the other reports, this one carried no royal seal. He placed it quietly on the table. “There is another possibility.”

The Chancellor looked at the file. “Who?”

The official opened it. “Ronan.”

Lord Beaumont frowned. “Who is Ronan?”

“A detective.”

The answer was met with silence.

“A detective?” Lord Beaumont repeated. “Yes. From where? East London.”

Lord Beaumont's expression became even more doubtful. “What family? None. What noble house? None. What political connections? None.”

Lord Beaumont stared at the official. “You are suggesting an unknown detective from East London should investigate my son's murder?”

“I am suggesting that his lack of connections may be exactly what we need.”

The Chancellor reached for the file. He opened it and began reading. “Twenty-seven years old. Orphan. Lives alone. No known noble connections. No political affiliation. No significant financial influence.”

The Chancellor looked up. “And his experience?”

The official answered. “He has handled small investigations for years. Missing property. Missing persons. Local disputes. Cases that often receive little attention from the official authorities.”

Lord Beaumont looked unimpressed. “My son was not a missing cat.”

The official did not react. “No, my lord.”

“Then why him?”

“Because he knows the parts of London that people like us rarely see. He knows the poor districts. He knows the streets. He knows people who would never speak freely to a Palace official. And most importantly…” He closed the file halfway. “People do not expect much from him.”

Lord Beaumont stared at him. The official's meaning was clear. An investigator from the Palace would attract attention. A famous detective would attract attention. A noble investigator would attract even more. But Ronan? He was almost invisible to the people who held power.

The Chancellor looked down at the file again. “That could be useful.”

Lord Beaumont shook his head. “It could also be dangerous.”

“Yes.” The official did not disagree. “But every choice carries a risk.”

The room fell silent again. Outside the tall windows, a carriage passed along the Palace road. For a moment, the sound of its wheels could be heard through the glass.

Then Lord Beaumont spoke. “If this detective fails…” He stopped. The official looked at him. Lord Beaumont's voice became firm. “Who will take responsibility?”

The Chancellor answered before anyone else could. “The Crown will.”

Lord Beaumont looked toward him. “And if he succeeds?”

The Chancellor's expression remained calm. “Then we will have the truth.”

Lord Beaumont looked back at the thin file. Ronan. A name he had never heard before. A man with no title. No powerful family. No political protection. No place among the people sitting around this table. Yet somehow, the Royal Palace was considering placing his son's murder in that man's hands.

One of the council members finally spoke. “Perhaps that is exactly why he should be chosen.” Lord Beaumont looked toward him. The man continued. “The killer already knows that the Palace will investigate. If we send someone expected, whoever did this will know what to expect. But if we send someone unexpected…” He left the sentence unfinished.

The Chancellor understood. So did everyone else.

Lord Alistair Ashford had remained silent throughout the discussion. His eyes rested on the file. “Ronan.” He repeated the name quietly.

The others looked toward him. “Have you heard of him?” the Chancellor asked.

Alistair shook his head. “No.” His answer was simple. Nothing more.

The Chancellor nodded. Then he looked around the table. “Are there any objections?”

Nobody spoke. Lord Beaumont remained silent for several seconds. Finally, he placed both hands flat against the table. “Bring him in.”

The Chancellor looked toward the investigation official. “You heard him.”

The official bowed. “Yes, Your Grace.”

“Bring Ronan to the Royal Palace.”

The official nodded. “When?”

The Chancellor looked toward the tall windows. “Today.”

The official bowed again. “I will send someone immediately.”

Lord Beaumont rose from his chair. His expression remained hard. “I want this investigation taken seriously.”

“It will be.”

“I want the murderer found.”

“You have my word.”

Lord Beaumont turned toward the door. “And when you find him…” He stopped. His voice was low. “Do not let him escape justice.”

The door opened. Lord Beaumont left the chamber. One by one, the other members of the council began to leave as well. The room slowly became empty. Only a few documents remained on the table. Among them was the thin file carrying a single name. Ronan.

A man who had spent most of his life unnoticed. A man who had never imagined that the Royal Palace would ever have a reason to remember his name. And yet, somewhere outside those walls, a royal servant was already preparing to find him.

Ronan's ordinary life was about to end. He simply did not know it yet.

The rain had stopped by the time the Royal Council finished its meeting. But in the poorer streets of London, the morning remained grey and damp.

Ronan had no idea that his name had just been spoken inside the Royal Palace. He had no idea that the most powerful people in the kingdom had decided to place a murder investigation in his hands.

For him, the morning had begun like any other. His small house was quiet. Too quiet. The fire in the fireplace had nearly gone out during the night, leaving only a few weak pieces of coal glowing beneath the ash.

Ronan sat at his old wooden table. His coat was hanging over the back of the chair, still carrying a little of the dampness from the previous evening. Beside him lay the newspaper he had bought that morning. The same story occupied almost the entire front page.

LORD ALDRICH BEAUMONT FOUND DEAD ON LONDON BRIDGE

Ronan had already read it twice. He still didn't like what he was reading. The newspaper gave very little information about the actual murder. Most of it was speculation. Rumors. Political concerns. Descriptions of the crowds gathering around London Bridge. And, of course, the strange playing card that had been found beside the body.

Ronan leaned back in his chair. His fingers moved toward the silver pocket watch in his coat. He took it out and opened the cover. Tick. Tick. Tick. The familiar sound filled the quiet room. He looked at the hands. Morning was already moving forward.

Ronan closed the watch and placed it beside the newspaper. He stood. There was very little food in the house. A small piece of bread remained on the table. He picked it up.

Before he could take a bite, he heard something outside. Footsteps.

Ronan stopped. The footsteps were coming closer. He looked toward the door. The sound was unusual for his street. Most people who came through this part of London walked quickly. They knew better than to stay outside for too long. But these footsteps were slow. Calm. Measured.

Ronan put the bread down. The footsteps stopped directly outside his house. For a moment, there was silence. Then came three knocks. Knock. Knock. Knock.

Ronan frowned. Nobody usually visited him this early. He moved toward the door. His hand rested on the handle. He opened it.

A man stood outside. Ronan immediately noticed that he did not belong there. The man wore a dark formal coat that was almost completely untouched by the mud surrounding the street. His shoes were polished. His gloves were clean. A small silver insignia was attached near the collar of his coat.

Behind him stood a black carriage. It looked completely out of place among the broken walls, narrow houses, and muddy streets.

Ronan looked at the man. Then at the carriage. Then back at the man. “Yes?”

The man gave a polite bow. “Mr. Ronan?”

Ronan hesitated. “Yes.”

“My name is Thomas Hale.” The man reached inside his coat and produced a small identification card. “I am a servant of the Royal Palace.”

Ronan stared at him. For a moment, he thought he had heard incorrectly. “The Royal Palace?”

“Yes, sir.”

Ronan looked behind the man again. The Palace carriage was still waiting. He looked back at Thomas. “Why would the Royal Palace send someone here?”

Thomas glanced briefly around the street. His eyes moved over the cracked walls, the narrow doorways, the dirty pavement, and the small houses pressed closely together. He tried not to show his reaction. But he couldn't completely hide it. “What a terrible place,” he murmured.

Ronan heard him. He raised an eyebrow. Thomas immediately straightened. “My apologies.”

Ronan looked at the carriage again. “What does the Palace want with me?”

Thomas reached into his coat once more. This time, he produced a sealed letter. He held it toward Ronan. “You have been formally summoned to the Royal Palace.”

Ronan did not take the letter immediately. “Summoned?”

“Yes, sir.”

“For what?”

“I was not given permission to discuss the matter.”

Ronan looked at the sealed letter. The Royal seal was pressed into the red wax. He had never held anything carrying that seal before. Slowly, he accepted it. The weight of the letter seemed strangely heavy in his hand. He broke the seal. His eyes moved across the words. He read them once. Then again. The Royal Palace requested his immediate presence.

Ronan lowered the letter. “This is real?”

Thomas gave a small nod. “Yes, Mr. Ronan.”

Ronan looked down at himself. His shirt was slightly wrinkled. His boots were old. His coat had seen better days. He looked toward the Palace carriage. “I can't exactly go to the Royal Palace dressed like this.”

Thomas glanced at him. Then, after a moment, he smiled politely. “The Palace is expecting you as you are.”

Ronan looked at him suspiciously. “That doesn't make me feel better.”

Thomas almost smiled. “You should bring your coat.”

Ronan turned back toward his house. He stopped at the doorway. His eyes moved across the small room. The old table. The single chair. The bookshelf filled with case files. The nearly empty cupboard. This place was not much. But it was his. He had spent years here. He knew every crack in the walls. Every loose floorboard. Every sound the old windows made when the wind became strong.

And now, without knowing why, the Royal Palace had come looking for him.

Ronan picked up his coat. Before leaving, he reached for his silver pocket watch. He placed it carefully inside his pocket. Then he locked the door behind him.

The street seemed different now. Perhaps it wasn't. Perhaps Ronan was simply looking at it differently.

The Palace carriage stood in the middle of the poor street like something from another world. Children had gathered nearby. Several shopkeepers were watching from their doorways. A few people whispered. Ronan heard one woman say, “That's a Palace carriage.” Another voice answered, “Who are they here for?”

Ronan pretended not to hear them. Thomas opened the carriage door. “After you, Mr. Ronan.”

Ronan stepped inside. The interior was far more comfortable than he expected. Soft seats. Clean fabric. Polished wood. Even the smell was different. For a man accustomed to narrow rooms and old furniture, the carriage felt almost luxurious.

Thomas sat opposite him. The driver climbed onto the front seat. A moment later, the carriage began to move.

Ronan looked through the window. His neighborhood slowly disappeared behind them. The small houses. The narrow streets. The broken walls. The crowded doorways. People carrying baskets. Children running through muddy roads. A woman washing clothes outside a building that looked as though it might collapse if the rain returned.

Ronan watched quietly. He had grown up around places like this. He had never considered them beautiful. But he knew them. He knew which streets flooded after heavy rain. Which baker would sell yesterday's bread cheaply. Which alley was safe during the day but dangerous after dark. Which families needed help. Which children slept hungry.

The carriage continued forward. Gradually, the streets began to change. The buildings became larger. The roads became wider. The pavement became cleaner. The houses were no longer pressed against one another. There were gardens. Iron gates. Tall windows. Large stone buildings with carefully maintained entrances.

Ronan leaned slightly toward the window. He watched as another world appeared around him. A bakery stood on the corner. The smell of fresh bread drifted through the air. Ronan's stomach immediately reminded him that he had eaten almost nothing. He watched a servant carry several warm loaves into a large house. He looked away.

The carriage continued. A little farther ahead, two men in expensive coats stood outside a grand building, speaking casually while a carriage waited beside them. Nobody seemed to be rushing. Nobody seemed worried about where their next meal would come from.

Ronan looked down at his own hands. His fingers were rough from years of work. He thought about the three copper coins he had spent on yesterday's bread. Then he looked outside again. The difference was difficult to ignore. On one side of London, people counted every coin. On the other, people seemed to live in a world where money was simply there.

Ronan didn't know what to make of it. He had seen both sides of London before. But he had never travelled between them in a Palace carriage.

The carriage turned onto a broad avenue. At the end of it, the Royal Palace slowly came into view.

Ronan stared. The enormous building rose above the surrounding streets. Tall stone walls. Massive gates. Rows of windows. Guards standing at the entrance. Everything about it seemed designed to remind anyone who approached that this was not an ordinary place.

Ronan sat back. His hand instinctively moved toward his pocket. He felt the outline of his old silver watch beneath the fabric. Tick. Tick. Tick. The sound calmed him.

The carriage slowed. The Palace gates opened. The carriage passed through. Ronan watched the guards as they stood aside. He had spent most of his life believing that places like this belonged to other people. Nobles. Ministers. Kings. Men whose names appeared in newspapers. Not to someone like him.

Yet here he was. Sitting inside a Royal carriage. Heading directly into the Palace.

Ronan looked through the window one last time. The gates closed behind him. And for the first time in his life, he realized that whatever was waiting for him inside the Palace was something far bigger than any case he had ever handled before.

The carriage came to a complete stop. Ronan looked through the window. The Royal Palace stood directly before him. For a few seconds, he simply stared. He had seen the Palace from a distance before, like most people in London had. But seeing it from the seat of a Royal carriage was different.

The enormous stone walls seemed even taller. The main entrance was guarded by several soldiers, all standing perfectly straight. Their uniforms were clean and carefully pressed, and each man carried himself with the kind of discipline Ronan had rarely seen outside the military.

Thomas opened the carriage door. “We have arrived, Mr. Ronan.”

Ronan stepped down. His boots touched the polished stone beneath the Palace entrance. He looked up. The building seemed to stretch endlessly above him.

Thomas began walking toward the entrance. Ronan followed. The moment he crossed through the Palace doors, the noise of London disappeared. There were no shouting newspaper boys. No market sellers. No carriage drivers arguing in the street. Only the quiet movement of servants and the distant sound of footsteps.

Ronan looked around. The floor was made of polished marble. Large paintings covered the walls. Golden lamps hung from the high ceiling. Everything was clean. Everything was carefully arranged. Nothing looked broken. Nothing looked forgotten.

Ronan suddenly became very aware of his old boots. He looked down at them for a moment. Then he continued walking.

Thomas led him through a long corridor. Several servants passed them. Some looked at Ronan with curiosity. Others immediately looked away. Ronan tried not to stare at anything for too long. He failed. A large painting caught his attention. A nobleman stood in the center of it, dressed in an elaborate black coat, surrounded by several members of his family. Ronan wondered how much the painting had cost. Probably more than he would earn in years. He quickly looked away.

They reached a large set of wooden doors. Two guards stood on either side. Thomas stopped. “Mr. Ronan has arrived.”

One of the guards opened the door. Thomas stepped aside. “You may enter.”

Ronan looked at him. “Are you not coming?”

“I have delivered you to the Palace.” Thomas gave a polite smile. “The rest is no longer my responsibility.”

Ronan looked at the open doorway. Then back at Thomas. “That isn't very comforting.”

Thomas smiled faintly. “You will be fine.”

Ronan took a breath. Then stepped inside.

The room was smaller than the council chamber he had imagined, but it was still larger than any room Ronan had ever owned. Several officials were waiting. At the center of the room stood the Chancellor. Beside him were two senior members of the Royal administration.

Ronan stopped a few steps from them. He wasn't sure whether he was supposed to bow. He had never been taught the proper way to behave inside a Royal Palace. So he simply stood straight.

The Chancellor looked at him. “Mr. Ronan.”

“Your Grace.” Ronan gave a slightly awkward bow. The Chancellor did not seem offended.

“Come closer.” Ronan approached.

One of the officials carried a thick leather folder. Another held a small wooden box. Ronan noticed both immediately. The Chancellor gestured toward the chair in front of the desk. “Sit.”

Ronan sat. The Chancellor remained standing. “You were brought here because the Royal Council has reached a decision concerning the murder of Lord Aldrich Beaumont.”

Ronan's expression changed. He had expected something important. He had not expected this. “My lord?”

“You are aware of the murder.”

“Yes.”

“You have already examined the public reports?”

“Yes.”

“And you have some knowledge of the crime scene?”

“Only what I saw personally.”

The Chancellor studied him. “That is one of the reasons you are here.”

Ronan said nothing.

The Chancellor turned toward the official holding the leather folder. “Give it to him.”

The official stepped forward. He placed the folder on the desk in front of Ronan. Ronan looked at it. A royal seal had been stamped into the leather. He slowly opened the cover. Inside were documents. Reports. Statements. A description of the crime scene. Information about Lord Beaumont. And several pages containing details that had not appeared in the newspapers.

Ronan's eyes moved quickly across the first few pages. The more he read, the more serious his expression became. This was not simply a local murder investigation. This was a Royal case.

The Chancellor spoke. “Effective immediately, you have been appointed to investigate the murder of Lord Aldrich Beaumont.”

Ronan looked up. “Me?”

“Yes.”

Ronan stared at the Chancellor. There was a short silence. “Why?”

The Chancellor's expression remained calm. “Because you have no political ties.”

Ronan said nothing.

“You are not connected to the noble families.” Another pause. “You have no personal interest in protecting anyone involved in this case.”

Ronan looked down at the file. “And you believe that makes me suitable?”

“We believe it gives you independence.”

Ronan slowly closed the folder. “I understand.”

The Chancellor looked toward the second official. The man stepped forward carrying the small wooden box. He placed it on the desk. Ronan looked at it. “What is that?”

“Something you will need.”

The official opened the box. Inside was a small metal badge. It was dark silver, polished carefully, with the Royal crest engraved at the top. Beneath it were the words: ROYAL INVESTIGATIVE BUREAU

Ronan stared at it. The Chancellor spoke. “The Royal Investigative Bureau has existed for matters requiring investigation beyond ordinary police authority.”

Ronan picked up the badge. It was heavier than he expected. “And I am part of it?”

“For this case, yes.”

Ronan looked at the badge again. He had spent years helping people without anyone officially recognizing him as a detective. Now the Royal Palace was handing him an official badge. For a moment, he didn't know what to say.

The Chancellor noticed his silence. “You will have access to Royal records where necessary. You will be permitted to question witnesses. You will have cooperation from the police. And you will report directly to the appropriate Royal authorities.”

Ronan nodded slowly. “What about Inspector Ward?”

The Chancellor looked at him. “You know Inspector Ward?”

“I know of him.”

“Inspector Elias Ward will cooperate with you.”

Ronan nodded. The Chancellor continued. “He will handle matters requiring police authority. You will handle the investigation.”

Ronan looked at the badge once more. Then something occurred to him. “Is there payment?”

One of the officials looked surprised. The Chancellor almost smiled. “Yes.”

“How much?”

The Chancellor answered without hesitation. “Ten gold coins per week.”

Ronan froze. For a moment, he thought he had misunderstood. “Ten…”

“Gold coins.”

Ronan stared at him. He slowly looked down at the badge in his hand. Then at the case file. Then back at the Chancellor. “Per week?”

“Yes.”

Ronan said nothing. His mind immediately began calculating. Ten gold coins. Every week. He normally earned only a few silver coins from the small investigations he handled. Sometimes he could go an entire month without having enough money to buy anything beyond the necessities. Ten gold coins every week was more money than he had ever imagined holding at once.

He tried to remain serious. He failed. A small smile appeared on his face. The official standing nearby noticed. Ronan quickly cleared his throat. “My apologies.”

The Chancellor looked amused. “There is no need.”

Ronan placed the badge carefully beside the file. “I accept.”

The Chancellor nodded. “Good.”

Ronan stood. He placed the badge inside his coat, then picked up the case file. For the first time, the weight of the investigation felt real. He had come to the Palace as an unknown detective. He was leaving with Royal authority.

But he knew that a badge could not solve a murder. The dead man on London Bridge was still dead. The killer was still somewhere in London. And the answers were still hidden.

The Chancellor spoke one final time. “Mr. Ronan.”

Ronan turned. “Do not allow the importance of this case to affect your judgment.”

Ronan looked at him. “I won't.”

“Follow the evidence.”

Ronan nodded. “That is what I intend to do.”

The Chancellor held his gaze for a moment. “Then begin.”

Ronan turned toward the door. His hand touched the badge inside his coat. The Royal Investigative Bureau. For the first time, he had an official name behind his work.

But before he could leave, another voice came from the corridor. “Mr. Ronan?”

Ronan turned. A tall man stood outside the doorway. He wore a dark police uniform beneath a long black coat. His face was serious, his grey eyes observant. Ronan recognized him immediately. Inspector Elias Ward.

Ward looked at Ronan. Then at the file in his hands. “I believe we have a murder to investigate.”

Ronan gave a small nod. “Yes.”

Ward stepped aside. “Then let's begin.”

Inspector Elias Ward led Ronan through another long corridor of the Royal Palace. Unlike the grand halls they had passed through earlier, this part of the building felt colder and quieter. The walls were plain. There were fewer paintings. The polished marble slowly gave way to dark stone flooring.

Ronan walked beside Ward, holding the leather case file against his chest. His new badge rested inside his coat. Every few steps, he could feel it against the fabric. It was a strange feeling. Only a few hours ago, he had been sitting in his small house, wondering what he would eat for breakfast. Now he was walking through the Royal Palace with an official badge and a murder case that had already become the concern of the entire kingdom.

Ward glanced at him. “You seem quiet.”

Ronan looked at him. “I am thinking.”

“About the case?”

“About the ten gold coins.”

Ward stopped walking. He stared at Ronan. Ronan looked back at him. “What?”

Ward sighed. “Nothing.”

Ronan continued walking. “I've never been offered ten gold coins for one week of work before.”

“I gathered that.”

“I could actually buy fresh bread.”

Ward looked at him again. “You are serious.”

“Very.”

A faint smile appeared at the corner of Ward's mouth. “You will get used to it.”

“I doubt that.”

They continued down the corridor. After a few moments, Ward's expression became serious again. “The money is not the important part.”

Ronan looked at him. “I know.”

“This case is different from anything you have handled before.”

Ronan tightened his grip on the file. “I know that too.”

Ward studied him for a moment. Then he nodded. “Good.”

They reached a large set of iron-framed doors. A small brass plate had been fixed beside them: FORENSIC DEPARTMENT

Ward pushed the door open.

A completely different smell reached Ronan. Not the smell of the Palace. Not polished wood. Not expensive perfume. This place smelled of chemicals, metal, and something colder that Ronan could not immediately identify.

The room beyond was large and brightly lit. Tables covered the center of the chamber. Glass containers stood on shelves. Medical instruments were arranged carefully beneath the lamps. Several assistants moved quietly from one table to another. Nobody spoke loudly. Everything seemed precise. Everything had a purpose.

Ward stepped inside. “Doctor Graves.”

No answer. Ward called again. “Doctor.”

A voice finally came from the far side of the room. “If you intend to interrupt me, Inspector, at least do it for a useful reason.”

Ronan looked toward the voice. A man stood beside a long examination table. He was tall and thin, dressed in a dark waistcoat beneath a white medical coat. His sleeves were rolled neatly to his forearms. His hair was dark, with a few strands of grey beginning to show. He was examining several papers while holding a small magnifying lens in one hand. He did not immediately look at Ronan.

Ward walked closer. “We have been sent by the Palace.”

That made the doctor stop. He slowly raised his eyes. His gaze moved from Ward to Ronan. Then to the file in Ronan's hands. “You are the new investigator.”

Ronan nodded. “Ronan.”

“I know.” The doctor placed the magnifying lens down. “Dr. Theodore Graves.”

Ronan had heard the name before. Graves was considered one of the most respected forensic doctors in London. He was not a man who wasted words.

Ward placed his hand on the edge of the examination table. “We need your report on Lord Beaumont.”

Graves looked toward the covered body lying beneath a white sheet. “You need more than my report.”

Ronan looked at him. “What do you mean?”

Graves pulled the sheet back slightly. “You need to understand how he died.”

Ronan's expression became serious. “Yes.”

Graves nodded. “Then look carefully.”

Ronan stepped closer. He had already seen the body on London Bridge. But seeing it here was different. There was no rain. No crowd. No shouting guards. No darkness. Only the harsh light of the forensic room. The reality of death was impossible to avoid.

Ronan stopped beside the table. Graves spoke calmly. “Lord Beaumont did not die immediately.”

Ronan looked at him. “His injuries?”

“Were severe.” Graves pointed toward several areas of the body without touching them. “There was significant blunt-force trauma. Some injuries were inflicted with considerable force.”

Ronan remained silent. Graves continued. “Pero those injuries alone do not explain everything.”

Ward folded his arms. “What else?”

Graves looked at Ronan. “There are signs that he was restrained.”

Ronan's eyes narrowed. “Restrained?”

“Yes. The marks indicate that his movement was deliberately limited before death.”

Ronan looked down at the evidence. “So he couldn't defend himself.”

“Correct.” Graves moved to another part of the examination table. “There are additional injuries. Some of them were inflicted after he had already suffered significant trauma.”

Ronan frowned. “After?”

“Yes.” Graves paused. “That tells us something important.”

“What?”

“The person responsible did not simply attack him and leave.”

Ronan looked at him. “They continued.”

Graves nodded. “There was deliberate, repeated violence.”

The room seemed quieter. Even the assistants working nearby appeared to have slowed down. Ward's expression hardened. “How long?”

Graves considered the evidence. “I cannot give you an exact period.”

“But?”

“It was not instantaneous.”

Ronan looked toward the doctor. “He was alive during some of it?”

Graves did not answer immediately. Then he said, “Yes.”

Ronan's jaw tightened. Graves continued. “There is also evidence of forced sexual assault.”

Ronan looked at him. The doctor spoke without emotion. “The injuries are consistent with deliberate sexual violence inflicted before death.”

Ronan's expression changed. He looked away from the body for a moment. Ward's face became colder. “Can you determine whether that caused his death?”

“No.” Graves shook his head. “The fatal injuries were more extensive.” He returned his attention to the body. “The immediate cause of death was severe internal trauma combined with blood loss.”

Ronan looked back at him. “Was it an accident?”

Graves looked at him as though the question itself was unnecessary. “No.” His answer was immediate. “There is nothing accidental about this.”

Ronan remained silent. Graves continued. “The injuries were deliberate. And whoever inflicted them understood that the victim was suffering.”

Ward asked, “Could one person have done this?”

Graves considered the evidence. “Yes.”

“Are you certain?”

“No.” He looked toward Ward. “I am certain that one person could have caused the injuries. But I cannot tell you whether another person was present.”

Ronan looked at the body again. “What about the time of death?”

“Late evening.”

“Before or after the rain?”

“Most likely before the heaviest rainfall.”

Ronan nodded slowly. That matched what he remembered from London Bridge.

Graves removed his gloves. “There is one more thing.”

Ronan looked at him. Graves folded his hands behind his back. “The violence was not random.”

Ward's eyes narrowed. “What does that mean?”

“It means the injuries do not resemble a simple attempt to kill.”

Ronan waited. Graves continued. “The victim was subjected to prolonged violence before death.” He looked directly at Ronan. “Whoever did this wanted him to suffer.”

Silence. Ronan looked down at the file in his hands. He thought about the body on London Bridge. The Fool card. The strange message. The hidden meaning behind the murder. But Graves had given him no name. No identity. No motive. Nothing that told him who had done it.

Only the method. Only the suffering. Only the certainty that Lord Beaumont had not simply been murdered. He had been deliberately punished before he died.

Ronan closed the case file. “Doctor.”

Graves looked at him. “Can you tell me anything about the person who did this?”

Graves shook his head. “No.”

“Nothing?”

“Nothing reliable.”

Ronan waited. Graves continued. “The injuries tell us what happened to the victim. They do not tell us who the attacker was.”

Ronan nodded. That was not the answer he wanted. But it was the answer he needed.

Ward looked toward the body one last time. “We will need the complete report.”

“You will have it.” Graves picked up his papers. “And Mr. Ronan?”

Ronan turned. “Yes?”

Graves looked at him with the same cold, analytical expression. “Do not confuse brutality with evidence.”

Ronan frowned slightly. Graves continued. “The body can tell you how a man died. It cannot tell you why.”

Ronan held his gaze. Then he nodded. “I understand.”

He turned toward the door with Ward beside him. As they walked away, Ronan's hand moved unconsciously toward the Royal Investigative Bureau badge inside his coat. His first official investigation had barely begun. And already, he had learned something he wished he hadn't.

Lord Aldrich Beaumont had not simply been killed. Someone had wanted him to suffer before he died. But why? That was the question Ronan carried with him as he left the Forensic Department.

And somewhere beyond the walls of the Royal Palace, London continued to whisper the same name. The Fool.
